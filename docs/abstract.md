# Who Is the Bus For?

## Student and Resident Spatial Imaginaries in Ann Arbor’s Transit System

**Name:** Tegran Grigorian

The city of Ann Arbor is described as a mid-sized college town, but it is also a city of long-term residents who live, work, and raise families year-round. This project asks how these two spatial imaginaries use the same space differently and how transit reveals the tension between them. Building on the concepts discussed in the White and Black Spatial Imaginaries, I treat the “student” and “resident” imaginaries as competing ways of seeing the city, each of which shapes and is shaped by the built environment its members inhabit.

My primary case is Ann Arbor’s two dominant transit systems: TheRide (AAATA) and the University of Michigan campus buses. I will analyze whom these systems are designed to serve, where their routes go and why that matters, when they run, and who gets left out. Rather than assuming the city serves students at the expense of residents, I will treat that as a hypothesis and test it. The university is also a major employer, and many residents, such as professors, depend on it, so I will examine how that complicates the divide. I will also complicate the binary itself, since graduate students, renters, homeowners, and longtime residents in different neighborhoods may relate to transit very differently.

To test this, I will combine on-site observation, archival work, and a large-scale simulation. I will ride routes at different times of day, noting what each stop serves, how reliable the buses are, and how crowded they get. I will also read AAATA board minutes and city council decisions on the future of specific lines. These findings will shape the simulation. I will generate 1,000 simulated personas based on census and housing data, assign each a home, a workplace or campus, and a weekly routine, and use public transit data and routing APIs to simulate their trips. The simulation will be repeated 100 times to create 100 distinct sample groups. I will then run statistical analyses of outcomes such as travel time, wait time, transfers, and trips the system cannot complete, comparing groups such as undergraduates, graduate students, renters, homeowners, and shift workers.

To show that Ann Arbor’s arrangement is not inevitable, I will run the same simulation in another university town. Finally, I will use the results to identify where the system fails the most people and design a revised transit map with new or changed routes. I will then rerun the simulation to see whether access actually improves.
